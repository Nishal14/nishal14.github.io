Mapping the Feasibility Boundary of Grokking in Transformers

How far "grokking" actually holds up under pressure.

February 7, 2026 7 min read

Neural networks sometimes behave in ways that feel almost counterintuitive. One of the strangest is grokking: a model can train for a long time while performing poorly on new examples, and then, almost suddenly, snap into near-perfect generalization.

That raised a question I couldn't stop thinking about: when does grokking actually happen, and when does it fail? Is it a rare accident, or are there clear conditions that make it possible?

In this project I tried to map out grokking's boundaries in small transformer models — running controlled experiments on simple arithmetic tasks, carefully varying how the data was structured, and watching where delayed generalization appears and where it disappears. The goal wasn't just to reproduce grokking, but to understand its limits.

What is grokking?

To understand grokking, it helps to think about how we usually expect learning to work. Normally, as training progresses, both training performance and generalization improve together — the model gets better at the task and gradually generalizes.

Grokking breaks that pattern. A model can reach very low training error early — essentially memorizing the training data — while still performing poorly on new examples, sometimes for a long time. Then, after many more training steps, it suddenly transitions to near-perfect generalization. The improvement is sharp and delayed, almost like a phase change.

One way to picture this is a student preparing for an exam. At first, he/she memorizes specific practice problems without really understanding the underlying ideas, and struggles with anything new. Then, after enough exposure, something clicks: they grasp the deeper pattern, and suddenly many new problems become easy. Grokking looks like a similar shift from memorization to structured understanding.

This delayed jump is surprising because it challenges the usual intuition that generalization improves smoothly. Instead, it suggests models can go through hidden stages — fitting the data shallowly at first, and only later discovering a more systematic solution.

The question I actually wanted answered

Once I started reading about grokking, a natural question came up: how fragile is this phenomenon? Does it happen under a wide range of conditions, or only in very specific setups?

Most demonstrations use carefully designed tasks where training and test data are closely related. I wanted to know what happens if you systematically change that relationship — where, exactly, is the boundary between settings where grokking appears and settings where it collapses?

So the goal wasn't to reproduce a known result, but to map the space around it. By gradually varying how much the training and test tasks overlapped, I could explore a spectrum — from familiar interpolation (new problems that look like ones the model practiced) to harder extrapolation (problems that go beyond anything it's seen). The central question: how does the structure of the problem shape the possibility of delayed generalization?

Setup

I trained small transformers on modular arithmetic — operations like addition or multiplication that wrap around at a fixed value, the same idea as clock arithmetic.

Modular arithmetic might sound abstract, but you can think of it like a clock. On a 12-hour clock, 9 + 5 doesn't equal 14 — it wraps around to 2. These kinds of tasks are simple enough to study in detail, but rich enough for interesting learning dynamics to appear.

The key move was varying how much the training data overlapped with the test data — in some settings covering most of the patterns the model would later be tested on, in others restricting it enough to force generalization to new combinations. That gave me a spectrum of regimes, from easy interpolation to hard extrapolation, each acting like its own environment for watching where delayed generalization emerged and where it broke down.

Three regimes, not a smooth curve

I expected something gradual — grokking just taking longer to show up as the task got harder. What I got instead was three distinct behavioral regimes.

Interpolation regime

High overlap between training and test problems. The model reused patterns it had already seen, learned quickly, and generalized early — accuracy rose steadily, no long delay. At first glance this looked like success, but internally the model wasn't really discovering the arithmetic rule. It was mostly stitching together familiar patterns. No dramatic phase transition, just gradual improvement.

Boundary regime

Reduced overlap. Test accuracy stayed low for a long stretch, hovering near zero — the classic setup for grokking. But the jump never fully materialized within the training window I used. This felt like standing at the edge of a cliff: the model was being forced off its shortcuts, but hadn't found a clean algorithmic solution yet.

Extrapolation regime

Minimal overlap. The model memorized the training set and never generalized — test accuracy stayed near chance no matter how long I trained. This wasn't delayed grokking. It was a hard wall.

The boundary is sharp, not gradual

The biggest surprise wasn't that these three regimes exist — it's how abrupt the transition between them is. I expected grokking to gradually take longer to show up as the task got harder. Instead there's a narrow boundary separating two very different worlds: one where interpolation lets the model generalize early, one where memorization simply fails outright. Push the task only slightly further toward extrapolation, and grokking doesn't slow down — it collapses. It lives in a fragile zone, not a wide one.

What's happening inside the model

Accuracy curves only tell you so much, so I also looked at the model's internal representations. In runs where grokking actually happened, the representations visibly simplified right as accuracy jumped — consistent with the model reorganizing around a cleaner, more compact rule. In the runs where grokking failed, the internal representations stayed messy throughout, and the model just kept memorizing without ever reorganizing. That's a clue: grokking isn't just about getting the right answers — it's about finding a simpler internal structure.

A rough theoretical picture

Putting this together: grokking requires a delicate balance — enough challenge to prevent easy shortcuts, but not so much novelty that the model gives up on finding a rule at all.

It seems to live near a feasibility boundary — a thin region where memorization becomes unstable and structured understanding finally wins.

One way to think about training is as a competition between two strategies. Memorization stores specific examples and patches them together — it works well whenever test problems resemble training ones, and interpolation makes it surprisingly powerful. Rule discovery instead finds a compact internal algorithm that works everywhere — for modular arithmetic, something close to the real underlying rule.

In high-overlap settings, memorization stays comfortable enough that the model never needs to reorganize — no dramatic transition, just steady improvement. In extreme extrapolation, memorization fails, but the distance to a working rule is too large to bridge, so the model just gets stuck. Grokking shows up in the narrow middle region where memorization is failing but rule discovery is still just barely reachable — that pressure is what produces the sudden phase transition. Seen this way, grokking isn't magic. It's a tipping point between two competing ways of solving a problem.

What this suggests

Generalization here depends heavily on task structure, not just training time. A small change to how you split train and test data can move a model from easy interpolation to total failure, with genuinely delayed generalization only appearing in a thin boundary region in between.

For beginners like me, that's a useful lesson: a model generalizing doesn't automatically mean it found a clean rule — sometimes it's just exploiting similarity in the data. True algorithmic understanding seems to need carefully balanced pressure, not just more training.

This doesn't solve grokking — it maps a slice of its landscape. But it points toward the idea that grokking lives right at a feasibility boundary between memorization and understanding, which is often exactly where the most interesting behavior in machine learning shows up. And that's exactly where I want to keep exploring.
