Introduction to Mechanistic Interpretability

Opening the black box instead of just staring at its outputs.

February 1, 2026 8 min read

AI is everywhere right now — new models, new tools, new claims about what they can and can't do seem to show up every week. Like a lot of people, that made me curious. Not just about what these systems can do, but about how they actually work underneath.

At first that curiosity was pretty vague — I trained models, used them in projects, and moved on. But the more I worked with neural networks, the less satisfying that felt. If a model solves a problem correctly, what does that actually mean? Is it following some internal procedure, or just matching patterns that happen to work?

That question eventually led me to mechanistic interpretability — a field that tries to understand neural networks by opening them up and studying what their individual parts do, instead of treating the whole thing as a black box. Rather than just checking outputs, it asks causal questions: what happens if I remove this component? What information is represented here? Which parts actually matter for a given behavior?

I explored these questions through a small project on transformers and arithmetic — part genuine curiosity, part wanting to build something concrete, and part, if I'm honest, wanting a solid project for college applications.

What follows is mechanistic interpretability the way I came to understand it — as a beginner, building small models, breaking them on purpose, and slowly forming intuitions about what's going on inside.

The question underneath the jargon

Before learning any terminology, I needed one plain question to anchor everything.

When a model solves a problem correctly, what is it actually doing internally?

Take an expression like (3 + 5) * 2. To a person, the structure is obvious — the parentheses mean 3 + 5 gets evaluated first, then multiplied by 2 (basic BODMAS/PEMDAS). We group the expression and apply rules we've internalized without even thinking about it.

A transformer doesn't get that for free. It's a model that reads sequences and learns how different parts relate to each other — it doesn't see a grouped expression, just a sequence of tokens: "(", "3", "+", "5", ")", "*", "2". That's it. Nothing in the input tells it which tokens belong together, or which operation should happen first.

This is exactly where parentheses get interesting. They introduce hierarchy into an otherwise flat sequence, so to solve the problem correctly the model somehow has to track:

  • where a parenthesis opens,
  • where it closes,
  • which tokens fall inside it,
  • and how operations inside parentheses should be handled differently from those outside.

In other words, it needs to reason about scope and nesting, not just local patterns.

To sharpen the question further, I trained the model under one more constraint: final-answer-only supervision. It never saw intermediate steps, never was told to evaluate 3 + 5 first — only the full expression and the correct final answer.

So the real question became: if a transformer is trained purely on final answers, does it still develop internal mechanisms that track structure — like parenthesis depth and scope — on its own?

That turned out to be a great setting for learning mechanistic interpretability: simple enough to fully understand, but structured enough to expose whether the model had learned something deeper than surface-level patterns.

What mechanistic interpretability actually asks

At a high level, mechanistic interpretability means opening up a model and asking:

What is actually going on inside this thing when it makes a decision?

Most of the time we judge AI only by its outputs — if the answer's right, we're satisfied; if it's wrong, we train it more. That treats the model like a black box: input in, output out, everything in between hidden. Mechanistic interpretability does the opposite. Instead of "did the model get it right," it asks:

  • Which parts of the model were responsible?
  • What information was being represented internally?
  • Are there specific components that handle specific sub-tasks?

In other words, it stops treating the model as magic and starts treating it as a system made of parts — parts you can inspect, test, and even break on purpose.

Why this task was a good fit

The arithmetic task I used was intentionally simple:

  • Expressions like 3 + 5, (2 + 4) * 3, or ((1 + 2) * 3) + 4
  • Only basic operators: addition, subtraction, multiplication
  • Parentheses limited to shallow nesting during training

Most importantly, the model only ever saw the final answer — no hints about order of operations, no explicit rules. It never saw anything like "evaluate inside the brackets first." If it picked up that behavior, it had to emerge on its own.

That's exactly what makes a small, fully-understood task useful here: the structure is non-trivial, and anything I find inside the model is meaningful rather than accidental.

What does "looking inside" mean?

So how do you actually look inside a transformer? Instead of treating it as one object, you break it into components:

  • Layers
  • Attention heads
  • Internal activations (numbers flowing through the network)

Each attention head is basically a tiny pattern detector — some might focus on nearby tokens, others on far-away ones, some on numbers, others on symbols like ( and ). Mechanistic interpretability asks: is any head doing something recognizable? Can we find heads that care about structure rather than surface patterns?

To answer that, I used three tools, each giving a different kind of evidence:

  • Ablation
  • Linear probing
  • Out-of-distribution testing

1. Ablation — breaking parts on purpose

Ablation is exactly what it sounds like: you deliberately disable part of the model and see what breaks. I selectively turned off individual attention heads and measured accuracy on flat expressions versus parenthesized ones. A head that barely mattered for flat expressions but caused a real drop on parenthesized ones was doing something specifically structural — not just general computation. That's causal evidence: the model isn't just correlated with parentheses, it actually relies on certain components to handle them.

2. Linear probing — checking what's actually represented

A different question: does the model explicitly represent parenthesis depth internally?

To test that, I froze the trained model, pulled out its internal activations, and trained a tiny linear classifier to predict parenthesis depth from them. If a simple linear model can decode it well, that information is sitting in a clean, usable form — not buried somewhere tangled. Individual attention heads encoded it moderately well on their own; the combined residual stream encoded it extremely well, which suggests the structure is integrated across the model rather than owned by any one part.

3. Out-of-distribution testing — checking it's not just memorization

Training only used expressions with nesting depth ≤ 2 — things like 3 + 5, (2 + 4) * 3, (1 + (2 * 3)), and ((4 + 5) * 2). At test time I gave the model unseen depth-3 expressions, like ((1 + 2) * (3 + 4)), (2 * (3 + (4 * 5))), and (((1 + 2) * 3) + 4) — familiar tokens, new structure. If the model were just memorizing, it should fail here. Instead, when I removed the attention heads I'd already flagged as structure-sensitive, performance on these deeper expressions dropped again — good evidence those heads were doing real, generalizing work, not something incidental.

What it adds up to

This project isn't really about arithmetic. It's a clean, controlled environment for asking a bigger question: do networks trained only on final outcomes still build internal structure? Here, the evidence says yes — the model developed attention heads causally involved in tracking structure, encoded abstract information like parenthesis depth in a usable form, and applied that machinery to structures it had never seen during training.

Representation, causality, and generalization all pointing the same way matters a lot if you care about reasoning in bigger systems — and about building AI you can actually trust because you understand it, not just because it usually works.

What surprised me

How concrete this all is. It's not vague hand-waving about "the model understands X" — you can point at specific components, turn them off, measure the effect, quantify what information is present, and test whether a mechanism actually transfers to new inputs.

The other thing that stuck with me: correct behavior doesn't guarantee correct reasoning. A model can land on the right answer through a shortcut or convenient memorization (kind of like humans, honestly). Mechanistic interpretability forces you to actually ask what the model is relying on, instead of assuming.

Working on a small, fully-controlled problem was a feature, not a limitation — because I understood the task completely, anything I found inside the model was checkable, not speculative. My takeaway for anyone starting out: you don't need a huge model or a huge dataset to do meaningful work here. Start small, ask concrete questions, and don't be afraid to open the black box. That's where the real learning happens.
